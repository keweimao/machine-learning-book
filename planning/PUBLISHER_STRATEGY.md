# Publisher Strategy and Proposal Roadmap

Date: 2026-08-10

## Recommendation

Approach **Cambridge University Press first**, because there is already a warm
relationship, Cambridge has a strong global textbook and course-adoption program,
and the earlier discussion suggests willingness to consider a print book alongside
an open web edition.

This recommendation is conditional on receiving written agreement on the open
website. Cambridge's standard Green Open Access policy explicitly does not apply to
textbooks, so a normal textbook contract would not by itself protect the right to
keep the full book online. The proposal should therefore begin with a short rights
and list-positioning conversation before a full formal review.

Approach **MIT Press second** if Cambridge cannot provide an acceptable web-edition
carve-out, requires an unaffordable open-access charge, or sees too much overlap
with its newly expanded introductory machine-learning list. MIT is arguably the
strongest intellectual and design fit, and its current computer-science editor
explicitly seeks machine-learning/AI textbooks for next-generation courses. Its
open-access record is excellent, but no-fee OA should not be assumed for a
textbook: Direct to Open is described primarily for scholarly monographs, while
several recent OA computing textbooks acknowledge outside funding.

Use **CRC Press/Taylor & Francis** as the strongest conventional textbook fallback
and **Springer Nature** as the strongest global-library/OA-by-fee fallback.

Do not lead with Manning, O'Reilly, or No Starch for this project. They can be
excellent technology publishers, but their product models are more practical,
tool-centered, and trade/professional than this theory-led college textbook, and
they do not naturally support a permanently open full-text edition.

## Book positioning for acquisition editors

Do not pitch the project as simply “another introduction to machine learning.”
Both Cambridge and MIT already have crowded machine-learning textbook lists.

Pitch it as:

> An introductory, information-centered machine-learning textbook that shows how
> data representations, probability, information, geometry, learning models,
> language, search, and graphs work together in the progression from data to
> information and meaning. A theory-first print narrative is paired with a
> versioned open web edition and a cross-linked Practice and Projects pathway.

Recommended working title for the proposal:

**Machine Learning: From Data to Information and Meaning**  
*Foundations, Models, and Practice*

The current “Quest for Information and Meaning” title can remain as a creative
alternative, but “From Data to Information and Meaning” communicates the
pedagogical progression more immediately to instructors and acquisitions teams.

### Primary audience

- Undergraduate students in an introductory machine-learning, data-science,
  information-science, or data-mining course.
- Students who have introductory programming and high-school/early-college
  mathematics but need probability, linear algebra, and information concepts
  developed in context.
- A one-semester core path, with optional advanced and Practice sections.

### Secondary audience

- Master's bridge courses and interdisciplinary graduate programs.
- Advanced high-school students and independent learners.
- Information-retrieval/NLP courses seeking a broader machine-learning foundation.

### What the book is not

- Not a graduate learning-theory reference.
- Not a framework-specific Python cookbook.
- Not a deep-learning architecture encyclopedia.
- Not a general artificial-intelligence survey.

### Distinctive features to emphasize

1. A coherent data → representation → probability/information → learning → meaning
   progression.
2. Information theory, IDF, divergence, and the author's Least Information Theory
   perspective as a distinctive conceptual thread.
3. Text, retrieval, and graphs as central applications—not late miscellaneous
   appendices.
4. Classical models and modern representation learning in one introductory arc.
5. A theory-first reading volume plus a cross-linked Practice and Projects pathway.
6. One-source Quarto production for accessible HTML, print PDF/LaTeX, and EPUB.
7. Classroom-tested explanations reconstructed from many years of courses and
   approximately 76.5 hours of the author's recorded teaching.
8. A public, versioned site that supports corrections, executable examples,
   accessibility, and adoption without making the print book obsolete.

## Why Cambridge remains first—but is not automatic

Cambridge publishes undergraduate and graduate textbooks across machine learning,
data science, information retrieval, and related mathematics. It supports digital
resources, examination copies, and courseware. The existing personal relationship
materially reduces acquisition uncertainty.

However, Cambridge's list has changed. Recent or forthcoming titles include
*Machine Learning with Python* (2025/26), *Introduction to Machine Learning: From
Math to Code* (2025), and another illustrated *Introduction to Machine Learning*
(2026). The proposal must show that this book is not competing primarily on “math
plus Python” or generic algorithm coverage.

The Cambridge conversation should answer two questions before substantial proposal
customization:

1. Does the current list still have room for an information-centered introductory
   ML textbook with a theory/Practice architecture?
2. Will Cambridge contractually permit the existing and future full web edition?

Cambridge currently states that Gold OA normally involves a Book Processing Charge;
its listed standard charge is US$13,000 for a monograph up to 120,000 words and 85
figures, with book-specific variation. It also states that its Green OA policy does
not apply to textbooks. These published policies do not prevent a negotiated model,
but they make the representative's earlier flexibility something that must be
documented precisely.

## Why MIT Press is the best alternative

MIT Press has the strongest conceptual alignment with the project's combination of
machine learning, information, computation, language, and meaning. It publishes
major textbooks in the Adaptive Computation and Machine Learning series and has a
long history of experimental open digital books.

The current Senior Acquisitions Editor for Computer Science, Susan Hartman,
specifically identifies machine learning and AI textbooks supporting
next-generation courses as an area of interest. MIT's standard process recommends
sending a short inquiry to the appropriate editor before sending attachments or a
full proposal.

The challenge is differentiation. MIT already publishes Alpaydın, Murphy,
Kelleher/Mac Namee/D'Arcy, Prince, and other major introductory or foundational
texts. The proposal must lead with the information-and-meaning progression,
retrieval/graph integration, and open theory/Practice architecture.

MIT's Direct to Open program is a significant strength, but its public description
focuses on scholarly monographs and edited collections. Recent OA textbooks such as
*Understanding Deep Learning*, *Fairness and Machine Learning*, and *Machine
Learning in Production* credit outside support. Ask directly whether the proposed
textbook could qualify for D2O, another OA fund, a sponsor-supported arrangement,
or an author-retained web edition.

## The proposed publishing model

Present the project as four coordinated products:

1. **Print textbook:** the stable, theory-first core with selected exercises and
   clear Practice pointers.
2. **Open web edition:** a versioned HTML edition of the core book, maintained from
   the same Quarto sources and linked to the version of record.
3. **Practice and Projects:** public notebooks, datasets, exercises, and optional
   advanced material, organized in parallel with the theory chapters.
4. **Instructor resources:** protected solutions, assignment variants, suggested
   syllabi, slides, and assessment materials.

This structure gives the publisher a saleable, stable textbook and adoption
package while preserving open access, accessibility, corrections, and executable
learning. The open site should be presented as product architecture and adoption
infrastructure—not merely promotional material.

For the print proposal, do not promise to print the entire Practice volume. A
manageable core textbook plus open Practice material will be easier to price,
adopt, revise, and keep current.

## Non-negotiable and negotiable terms

### Non-negotiable

- The existing public draft may remain online during review and development.
- The author may maintain a complete free HTML edition after publication.
- The author retains control of the Git repository, code, notebooks, datasets, and
  update workflow.
- Public code can carry an appropriate open-source license independent of the prose.
- The web edition may link to and identify the publisher's version of record.
- The author may correct errors and update web-specific material without waiting
  for a print edition cycle.
- Student-identifying teaching material and unlicensed third-party material will
  not be included.

### Negotiable

- Whether the web prose is identical to the print edition or a separately branded
  “open course edition.”
- The Creative Commons license. CC BY-NC-ND is a likely starting point because it
  is used in Cambridge, MIT, and Springer OA examples; code should normally use a
  separate permissive software license.
- Whether Cambridge/MIT hosts the OA version, the author hosts it, or both.
- Whether the site carries publisher design/branding.
- Embargo, if any, between print publication and the final open edition.
- Commercial eBook exclusivity, translation, audio, and derivative rights.
- BPC or sponsorship, but the author should not personally commit to a large BPC
  without institutional or external funding.

Keep the current public draft “all rights reserved” until the publishing route and
license are agreed. A premature Creative Commons license cannot later be withdrawn
from copies already distributed.

## Questions for the first Cambridge conversation

1. Given Cambridge's 2025–26 introductory ML titles, where would this project fit
   on the current list, and what positioning would avoid overlap?
2. Can the complete current GitHub Pages draft remain public during proposal review,
   contracting, peer review, and production?
3. Can the contract explicitly reserve the author's right to maintain a complete,
   free, updated HTML edition after publication?
4. Would Cambridge treat this as a conventional textbook with a negotiated web
   carve-out, a Gold OA textbook, or another publishing model?
5. If Gold OA is required, what exact BPC, funding routes, and license would apply
   to this textbook—not to a hypothetical monograph?
6. Can code, notebooks, exercises, datasets, and instructor resources remain in
   separate repositories/licensing arrangements?
7. What formats would production require? The author can deliver LaTeX, PDF, EPUB,
   Word, and Quarto source, but Quarto should remain the canonical authoring source.
8. What peer-review, approval, manuscript-delivery, and publication timetable is
   realistic?
9. What adoption, instructor-resource, and marketing support would Cambridge
   provide, and what would it expect from the author?

## Full proposal package

Prepare one master proposal and then tailor its opening and competition discussion
for each publisher.

1. **Two-page executive prospectus**
   - Problem and teaching need.
   - Information-centered solution.
   - Audience, prerequisites, and course types.
   - Print/web/Practice product architecture.
   - Current completion and delivery date.
2. **Annotated table of contents**
   - Learning objectives and role of every chapter.
   - Approximate words, figures, exercises, and current completion.
   - Core versus optional sections.
3. **Pedagogy and adoption plan**
   - One-semester and two-semester course paths.
   - Theory-to-Practice cross-references.
   - Exercises, projects, summaries, key terms, and instructor resources.
   - Evidence from current student pilots and prior courses.
4. **Competition analysis**
   - Cambridge: Wang, Bhatia, Shelton, and relevant data-science texts.
   - MIT: Alpaydın, Murphy, Kelleher et al., Prince, and relevant open textbooks.
   - Widely used non-press competitors, including ISLR-style and open online books.
   - A feature-by-feature comparison, not general praise or criticism.
5. **Sample materials**
   - Chapter 5 as the distinctive conceptual sample.
   - Chapter 7 or 12 as the core machine-learning/application sample.
   - One polished Practice activity demonstrating the web workflow.
   - Working web and PDF links.
6. **Market evidence**
   - Course titles, level, approximate enrollments, and departments.
   - Five to ten potential external adopters/reviewers.
   - Student feedback from the summer pilot.
7. **Author qualifications**
   - CV, courses taught, research in information retrieval/information theory, and
     evidence of sustained teaching-material development.
8. **Production specification**
   - Target 85,000–100,000 core words.
   - Estimated figures/tables/equations.
   - Quarto-to-LaTeX/PDF/EPUB/Word workflow.
   - Accessibility, alt text, code testing, and figure-provenance process.
9. **Rights and open-edition memorandum**
   - One page describing exactly what remains open, who hosts it, licensing,
     versioning, code/data rights, and the publisher-version link.
10. **Delivery schedule and risk controls**
    - Core-first scope.
    - RL and detailed architecture labs deferrable.
    - Figure/permissions and code modernization gates.

## Best sample chapters

Recommended proposal samples:

1. **Chapter 5: Information, Entropy, and Divergence**—the most distinctive
   conceptual contribution after the P44–P48 transcript revision.
2. **Chapter 12: Search and Retrieval**—demonstrates the author's subject
   authority, application breadth, and transition from classical to semantic
   retrieval.
3. **Optional third sample: Chapter 7, Classification**—shows that the manuscript
   also handles the expected introductory ML core.
4. **One Practice sample**—a short, executable, classroom-ready activity linked
   from one of the sample chapters.

Do not use a thin placeholder chapter or an overly framework-specific CNN/RNN lab
as a proposal sample.

## From inquiry to publication

### August 2026: editor qualification

- Reconnect with the Cambridge representative.
- Send the two-page overview, current TOC, working URL, and rights questions.
- Ask for a brief call and a decision on whether to prepare a full proposal.
- In parallel, identify five outside instructors willing to review the concept;
  do not ask for endorsements before they see polished samples.

### September–October 2026: proposal package

- Complete the Chapter 5 transcript-backed pilot.
- Polish Chapter 12 and one Practice sample.
- Prepare the master proposal, course maps, competition matrix, adoption evidence,
  and open-edition memorandum.
- Submit formally to Cambridge if the rights/list conversation is positive.
- If Cambridge is negative or materially uncertain, send the short MIT inquiry;
  send the full MIT proposal only if invited.

### October–December 2026: review plus manuscript alpha

- Continue the chapter-production roadmap while the proposal is reviewed.
- Respond to reviewer questions with revised samples/TOC, not wholesale reactive
  restructuring unless the editor identifies a clear market problem.
- By year end, target a complete, internally consistent public beta and publisher
  review package. Do not promise a 2026 print publication.

### 2027: contract, revision, and production

- Resolve peer review, editorial-board approval, and contract terms.
- Deliver the final manuscript and permissions/alt-text/code packages on the
  contractual schedule.
- Expect copyediting, typesetting, proof review, indexing, accessibility checks,
  metadata, advance cataloging, examination-copy preparation, and manufacturing to
  require substantial time after manuscript acceptance.

A proposal submission and strong beta by December 2026 are feasible. A finished
commercial print edition by December 2026 is not realistic; late 2027 or later is a
more credible outcome, depending on the publisher's review and production queue.

## Course adoption without becoming a marketer

The publisher should own catalog placement, metadata, distributor relationships,
conference exhibits, examination copies, library sales, and broad promotional
campaigns. The author should not promise to become a full-time marketer.

The minimum high-value author role is:

- Maintain the open site and correction channel.
- Recruit a six-to-ten-person instructor advisory/review group.
- Pilot the material in the author's courses and document improvements.
- Provide two suggested syllabi, instructor slides, and protected solutions.
- Make a small number of targeted launch presentations/webinars.
- Personally contact a limited set of colleagues who teach matching courses.
- Supply short, accurate descriptions and occasional publisher-requested content.

This is adoption development, not consumer marketing. It is also the kind of
evidence an acquisitions editor needs to justify a textbook contract.

At the proposal and contract stages, ask the publisher to identify its concrete
adoption plan: course-list research, examination copies, faculty outreach, sales
representation, international rights, conference presence, and expected author
activities. Avoid a contract whose commercial plan depends mainly on the author's
social-media following or continuous promotional output.

## Decision rule

Choose the publisher offering the best combination of:

1. Explicit permanent full-web-edition rights.
2. Serious developmental and technical peer review.
3. A credible college-course adoption program.
4. Affordable student print/eBook formats.
5. Professional production, metadata, distribution, and longevity.
6. A realistic schedule and limited, clearly defined author-marketing burden.

Prestige should break a tie; it should not override the open-edition right or the
ability to finish the book.
